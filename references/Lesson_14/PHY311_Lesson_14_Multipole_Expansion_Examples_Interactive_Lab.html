Multipole Expansion: Five Charge Distributions

An interactive, step-by-step laboratory. Predict the surviving moments, vary source geometry, compare exact and truncated potentials, and explain what remains visible far from a charge distribution.

The question behind every calculation

When you move far from a charge distribution, which details of its geometry survive? The multipole expansion answers this in an ordered sequence: net charge, dipole, quadrupole, and higher angular patterns.

Validity: This exterior expansion requires the observation radius r > Rmax, where Rmax encloses every source charge. The polar angle θ is measured from +z, as in the lecture.

Step 1 · What changes with geometry?

A moment is not an extra piece of charge. It is the charge weighted by its distance and angular position.

Step 2 · Prediction

If a distribution is reflected across the equatorial plane without changing its charge, which moments must vanish?

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
Reflection-even charge gives Qₙ = 0 for odd n; reflection-odd signed charge gives Qₙ = 0 for even n. The charge element dQ must always be normalized first. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

A point charge: only the monopole survives

Place a charge at the origin. Moving the observation point changes the potential, but cannot create a higher moment about this origin.

Step 1 · Geometry first

All source charge sits at r′ = 0. For n ≥ 1 the factor r′ⁿ vanishes; the undefined polar direction at the origin does not matter.

Step 2 · Origin matters

The same physical charge shifted away from the chosen origin generally acquires higher moments. The field is unchanged; its expansion about the origin is different.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
Only Q₀ survives for a point charge at the origin; changing r affects 1/r but cannot create angular dependence. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

The axial dipole: parity predicts the answer

Two opposite charges have zero net charge. Their signed positions reinforce the dipole moment and eliminate even moments.

Step 1 · Calculate the signed sum

Both charges have source radius d/2. Their polar angles are 0 and π, so Pₙ(1) = 1 and Pₙ(−1) = (−1)ⁿ.

Step 2 · Interpret the far field

The even moments cancel. The first surviving moment is Q₁ = qd, directed from negative to positive charge.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
The two contributions cancel for even n and add for odd n. At large r, the n = 1 term dominates. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

A spherical cloud: angular cancellation

Even when density varies with radius, spherical symmetry removes all exterior multipoles beyond the monopole.

Step 1 · Set up the volume integral

For any radial density f(r′), separate the radial and angular integrations. The total charge normalizes the radial integral.

Step 2 · Orthogonality removes detail

Use u = cos θ′, including the reversal of limits. Every n ≥ 1 angular integral vanishes, even for nonuniform radial density.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
Every higher angular pattern averages to zero over a sphere; the radial density affects total q, not exterior angular moments. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

A circular ring: negative quadrupole

Every source element lies in the equatorial plane, where the Legendre factor P₂(0) is negative.

Step 1 · Choose a line element

On the ring, r′ = a and θ′ = π/2. The azimuth varies, but the Legendre factor is constant.

Step 2 · Check the exact axis

The negative quadrupole coefficient is a geometric signature of equatorial charge, not a negative net charge.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
The ring has only even n. Q₂ is negative because P₂(0) = −1/2; the net charge Q₀ remains positive. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

A finite axial line: positive quadrupole

Compare the line and ring: both have positive net charge, but their different geometry reverses the sign of Q₂.

Step 1 · Split at the origin

The upper half has θ′ = 0; the lower has θ′ = π. Both have the same positive line density.

Step 2 · Compare with the ring

Odd moments vanish for both. On the polar axis P₂(±1) = +1, whereas on the ring P₂(0) = −1/2.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
The line also has only even n, but Q₂ is positive because P₂(±1) = 1. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.
EXPERIMENT 07 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Superposition: design a neutral quadrupole

Combine a positive spherical cloud and a negative equatorial ring. The monopoles cancel, leaving a quadrupole far field.

positive sourcenegative sourceobservation

Step 1 · Add moments, not charge elements

The cloud contributes only Q₀ = +q. The ring contributes Q₀ = −q and Q₂ = +qa²/2.

Step 2 · Identify the leading term

The monopole cancels, and reflection symmetry eliminates the dipole. The quadrupole therefore controls the far field.

Predict before revealing: What should happen to the dominant potential contribution when the observation distance doubles? How do symmetry and source geometry determine the first nonzero term?
Q₀ = 0, Q₁ = 0, Q₂ = qa²/2. The leading potential scales as r⁻³, not r⁻¹. In general the n-th potential term decreases by a factor 2−(n+1) when r doubles at fixed θ.

Consolidation: the reusable six-step method

Describe the source → identify Rmax → normalize dQ → calculate moments Qn → eliminate forbidden modes using symmetry → write and interpret the exterior potential. Before trusting a result, verify net charge, dimensions, convergence domain, and far-field decay.

Compare: Point and spherical cloud: monopole only. Axial opposite charges: odd moments only. Ring and centered line: even moments only, but opposite quadrupole signs. Neutral cloud + negative ring: quadrupole leading.
